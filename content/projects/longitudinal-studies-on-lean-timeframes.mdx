### Using dark data to map customer experience over time

<Columns>

<Column>

## Overview

I was the lead UX Designer in Hargreaves Lansdown's first multi-disciplinary Agile squad responsible for improving the customer experience of transferring ISAs and pensions.

### Challenge

<Challenge>

### To quickly uncover some of the "unknown unknowns" during a customer journey lasting anywhere from 1 month to 2 years.

</Challenge>

At the time, an average ISA or pension transfer was taking around 50 days, but in extreme cases over 2 years. Given our customers expect a transfer to take 3-5 working days (the same as a bank transfer) it was no surprise angry, frustrated and confused customers were calling the helpdesk.

Historically, no one was accountable for the transfers journey and as a result, we didn't know what communications we sent to clients, when we sent them or what could happen.

</Column>

## Approach

Ideally, I would have liked to use a diary study to understand customer experience across channels and how needs change over time but, due to the time constraints that come with Agile, it just wasn't feasible.

To get a comparable amount of insight - in a much shorter time - I proposed using [dark data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_data) (unused data from internal systems and call recordings) to map customer experience.

### Recruit the "participants"

Working with the team responsible for management information, I asked for a list of 50 customers who had;

- successfully transferred within the last 30 days,
- a transfer length of over 50 days, and
- contacted us at least 5 times by phone or email (regarding their transfer).

12 hours later, I received an excel document containing customer IDs and the dates of inbound and outbound contact across all channels.

### Refine the sample

Working with a Customer Relationship Management (CRM) system expert, I reviewed the customer journeys to refine the sample for further analysis. I wanted a broad (but representative) group of people covering:

- digital and paper transfers
- ISA and Pension transfers
- investment, cash and mixed portfolios
- relevant phone and email contact
- no "complex" investments (a known problematic edge case)

The initial sift reduced the initial list of 50 customers to around 20.

### Map high-level journeys

I transposed the data from the CRM into Excel to create high-level customer journeys documenting key touchpoints and events (application, stock received, cash received, transfer complete etc.), inbound and outbound digital communications, and the times and dates of calls.

I also reviewed the CRM data before the transfer (to identify contact relating to early information seeking/enquiry) and after the transfer (to understand goals).

### Add call data

20 customers with at least 5 calls per customer lasting between 3 and 55 minutes - That's a lot of gnarly data to try and make sense of.

So, we did what any healthy Agile squad would and swarmed on the problem.

Everyone in the team took responsibility for listening to and analysing calls. To normalise the output of 10 very different people, I provided a simple framework for recording calls.

- What did the customer say/ask?
- What was the call agent's response?
- What do you believe, based on the reaction of the customer, they thought?
- What do you believe, based on the reaction of the customer, they felt?

Listening to calls in the context of a customer journey had a huge impact on the team, and I recommend all teams (and stakeholders) do something like this regularly.

Firstly, introducing a concept like emotional granularity helps people become more aware and mindful of themselves and others - increasing emotional literacy is always a good thing.

<Figure src="/assets/e568f16f-253f-4611-a3b7-433607ff23a2/image.png">

I used [Robert Plutchik's wheel of emotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Plutchik#Plutchik's_wheel_of_emotions) to help the team articulate emotions consistently

</Figure>

More importantly, it gave the team a monumental amount of empathy for our customers and the problems they were facing. Occasionally, while we sat at our desks with headphones on documenting calls, someone would shout "you've got to hear this". At which point, we would gather around and recoil as we heard customers given misinformation, conflicting advice or disclosing the emotional and financial impact of delays. Powerful stuff.

### Add web analytics

The last layer of data to add was interactions with the website and mobile app. I was eager to know if any digital interactions confused clients or provoked calls as they could be easily addressed. I sent a list of the customers (and the time period of their transfer) and every page URL containing transfer related content and functionality to the team responsible for management information asking for the date and time of any page views.

12 hours later, I had another excel document containing page views to combine with the customer journeys.

### Add visual polish

One day I will find a way to automate converting ugly excel documents into beautifully compelling user journey maps. However, until then, I will continue to do it by hand in Omnigraffle or Miro.

### Stress-test the findings

Looking at such a small sample, I was conscious of sampling bias. In an effort to mitigate the risk of acting on biased data I walked as many people as possible through the customer journeys looking for feedback. This included helpdesk staff, operational staff, engineers and even directors.

My goal was to understand if the findings were representative of the larger customer base or just outliers.

## Outcome

<Metrics>
  <Metric label="Avoidable Contact" value="-63%" description="During first 3 days of transferring" />
  <Metric label="Digital Adoption" value="+42%" description="First time digital transfers overtook post" />
  <Metric label="Call Volume" value="-23%" description="Across customer journey" />
</Metrics>

The insight provided by this exercise proved invaluable and informed a significant proportion of our work and research going forward. Here are just a few of the things we learnt:

### Helpdesk behaviours were negatively impacting customer experience

When customers phoned with transfer queries, helpdesk agents would almost instinctively email (or post) paper forms to them. Paper forms are more difficult for users to fill in, require more operational processing, have a higher propensity for user error and delay transfers. Further investigation revealed helpdesk agents were financially incentivised to send these forms.

Future work saw us removing this incentive and defining a process for helpdesk agents to transfer ISAs and Pensions over the phone. Once the process was in place we saw customers' telephone transfers completing in minutes - not weeks.

### Email agent behaviours were negatively impacting customer experience

We uncovered a pattern of behaviour where responses to customer emails failed to answer customers questions. Instead, agents would talk about the process and the systems involved. As a customer, who has lost visibility of a £250,000 pension you don't want to know how a transfer "usually works" or "it's probably being processed by Origo". You need confidence your money is safe and to know when it will be back under your control.

We shared these findings with managers who used them to influence training.

### We had a mental model problem

We were alerted to a problem where customers with financial products containing investments and cash expected the cash to be transferred first.

> I understand you have to do some 'stuff' with my investments, but cash is simpler, right? Shouldn't it come first?

Having investments appear in their account without the cash was a significant cause of anxiety for customers.

Unfortunately, due to the way financial services operate across the industry, this is not the case. The cash portion of transfers always comes last as it can change (for example, accrue dividend payments during the transfer).

As any experience designer knows, there are two ways to deal with a mental model mismatch:

1. Change the system to conform to the users' mental model (usually expensive)
2. Change the users' mental model to conform to the system (usually tough)

Obviously, getting an industry to change its processes was out of scope for our squad, so we focused on changing users' mental models by adding content at key points in the journey to reset expectations.

### No news is not good news and clever words are not clever

We identified several opportunities to improve the customer experience by changing the communications we sent during the journey.

Firstly, the email telling customers they had successfully requested a transfer was sent 1-3 days after submitting the webform, resulting in customer anxiety. Further investigation revealed this was due to the manual intervention needed to process the transfer. Future work saw us move the responsibility for sending this email to the web tier giving customers instant feedback and reducing calls.

We also noticed we were not sending a number of key communications (most notably, telling customers their transfer had completed) and other (automated) communications focused on the event that triggered them (i.e. this investment has transferred), not the status of the entire transfer (i.e. this investment has transferred and these investments are being processed). We addressed these issues in later sprints.

Finally, many of our communications were just too complex. They contained industry jargon, acronyms and irrelevant information. On top of this, they were written in a formal style that made comprehension anywhere from tricky to next to impossible. Our content designer rewrote the offending communications in [Plain English](http://www.plainenglish.co.uk/how-to-write-in-plain-english.html) in an [inverted pyramid](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/) structure.

### That feeling you get when the CEO talks about a customer by name

During the exercise we uncovered one customer whose experience was so compelling, it's still talked about years later with people remembering her name. I won't go into the specifics of what happened, but when you witness your CEO passionately talking about a specific customer's needs and experience - motivating others to do better, you know you've done a good thing.

## Key learnings

This work reminded me that data doesn’t always come from usability tests or interviews—it’s often hiding in plain sight. By treating data more like a raw material than a finished product, we were able to craft a compelling, evidence-led view of the end-to-end experience in record time.

I also saw the impact of storytelling first-hand: when your CEO is quoting a customer’s words from your journey map, you know you’ve hit a nerve. It taught me that one of a UX designer’s superpowers is to amplify voices that would otherwise go unheard—and that making those voices feel real, human, and specific can drive genuine change.

Finally, it reaffirmed the importance of emotional intelligence in design work. By helping the team develop shared emotional vocabulary, we didn’t just analyse a journey…we felt it.
