## Overview

I was the lead UX Designer for the re-design, development and delivery of a large, business-critical, Government service. The service is used by 150,000 internal users who perform over 40 million transactions a year.

### Challenge

<Challenge>

### To increase system security with minimal disruption to users—by delivering a 2FA solution through agile, user-centred design.

</Challenge>

This was a highly complex piece of work with challenging design problems and considerations, for example:

- 2FA cards had to be posted to users, however, we didn’t know their addresses as they weren’t migrated from the legacy systems,
- the cards had to be registered by users as we couldn’t risk posting pre-registered cards,
- there were a lot of edge and corner cases to consider and design for (forgotten cards, lost cards, faulty cards, incorrect addresses),
- we had to ensure the solution was inclusive and catered for people who couldn’t operate physical cards (due to physical or motor impairments),
- as the cost of procuring and administering cards was high, we had to be mindful to protect against duplicate orders and abuse

## Approach

### Understanding the problem

We needed to add two-factor authentication (2FA) to a large, business-critical Government service used by 150,000 internal users. But this wasn’t just a technical upgrade — we had to make it secure, inclusive, and usable for people working in MOT garages all over the country. And we had to do it fast.

What made this even harder was that we didn’t have all user’s postal addresses. Cards couldn’t be pre-registered. And we had to plan for all the edge cases: lost, broken, or forgotten cards; incorrect addresses; physical or motor impairments. The stakes were high — and so were the costs of doing it wrong.

### Designing through constraints

I brought together researchers, designers, content specialists, analysts, and technical leads to co-create the solution. It was truly collaborative: from testing paper and code prototypes to drafting the letters that would be sent alongside cards.

A big part of the challenge was guiding users to update their own personal details without making it feel like admin. We did this by reframing the value — showing them that securing their account was in their own interest. This helped us cleanse the user data and reduce delivery failures.

<Figure src="/assets/projects/improving-service-security-through-2fa/research-and-design.png" />

### Prioritising the right things

Together with product owners and business analysts, we broke the complexity down into manageable parts. We prioritised features that offered the most business value and delayed edge cases — tracking them instead with analytics to see if they warranted future iteration. This focus helped us deliver faster without cutting corners.

### Helping users adapt

We didn’t stop at the UI. We created blog posts, letters, and videos to guide users through the change — recognising that even a small security step can feel like a big shift when it affects daily workflows.

### Outcome-focused iteration

As we rolled out the cards in stages, we closely monitored support metrics and user feedback. This let us adjust the experience in near real-time and build confidence in the change. By the end of the rollout, 150,000 users had successfully adopted the new system — with minimal disruption and a stronger foundation for service security.

## Key learnings

This was a reminder that inclusive design isn’t something you layer on at the end—it has to shape your thinking from the start. Designing for people who couldn’t physically use the 2FA cards forced us to look beyond the “happy path” and consider a wide range of human limitations, not just technical ones.

And finally, I learned that physical security measures deserve just as much design attention as digital ones. Getting the details right—how the cards were distributed, how they were registered, what the accompanying letters said—made all the difference in helping people adopt the new system with confidence.
