### Prototyping a personalised and contextualised NHS.UK

<Columns>

<Column>

## Overview

As part of a small multidisciplinary team at NHS Digital, I contributed to an exploratory initiative to prototype a more personalised and contextual NHS.uk experience. Commissioned by the Head of Design, the goal was to create a high-fidelity prototype that could provoke reaction and shape the direction of the NHS’s digital strategy.

### Challenge

<Challenge>

### To provoke strategic conversations about personalisation and user context by prototyping a joined-up NHS.uk experience.

</Challenge>

NHS leadership knew teams were working in silos, developing isolated services that didn’t reflect the holistic needs of users. This prototype aimed to challenge that — to help stakeholders see what a more joined-up, location-aware experience might look like across multiple services and touchpoints.

## Approach

### Investigating the brief

At first, I was surprised — even sceptical — about being asked to build a prototype in six weeks with no domain knowledge or direct user insight. So I challenged the Head of Design which led to one of the most enlightening conversations of my career. 

He explained that this wasn’t a prototype for testing usability — it was a **prototype to provoke**. Drawing on Houde and Hill’s framework and Ruecker’s prototype taxonomy, he clarified that our job was to help stakeholders see what was possible and respond to it.

### Co-creating the journey

We began by co-creating a realistic, end-to-end journey that crossed multiple NHS services. Using established design principles, we aligned around a user-centred narrative that highlighted key goals and tasks. This formed the backbone of the experience.

We shared this narrative with senior stakeholders and iterated quickly based on their feedback. Once we had their buy-in on the journey structure, we moved into flow-level design — mapping out what each part of the experience would require and how it would unfold.

### Scoping the technology

To keep the prototype grounded in reality, we mapped potential technology enablers — what systems or integrations would need to exist for the experience to work. This gave us a high-level service blueprint that connected the interface to the wider organisational capability.

### Designing the Service Finder

It was my responsibility to deliver a key interaction: helping users find relevant services based on where they are and what they might need. This wasn't just a visual redesign — it was about reframing the experience around user context and intent.

I started by prototyping a location-aware landing experience that could dynamically display nearby services, tailored based on the user’s status (e.g. logged in or not), preferences, and usage history. This included contextual prompts like “Find services near me,” supported by clear entry points to local GPs, pharmacies, and clinics.

To convey a sense of trust and value, I designed personalised cards that surfaced relevant services alongside practical guidance — things like travel information, eligibility reminders, and suggested next steps. These elements helped show what account-based personalisation could unlock in a real-world setting.

The interface was fully responsive and designed to scale — visually consistent across mobile and desktop. To ground the work in reality, we used real NHS service data wherever possible and shaped the experience around user needs identified in previous NHS research.

### Staying aligned

Even though we were working in smaller streams, we met twice weekly to critique work and maintain cohesion. These sessions were invaluable — they helped us challenge each other’s thinking, avoid duplication, and keep the vision consistent across the prototype.

## Outcome

The prototype achieved what it was designed to do: provoke reflection and open up strategic conversations. It helped stakeholders see — perhaps for the first time — that while NHS teams were designing services, they weren’t designing experiences.

The work didn’t just showcase a personalised interface — it exposed the fragmentation across NHS services and highlighted the value of a joined-up, user-centred approach. As a result, leadership began to reframe their thinking, moving away from isolated service delivery and toward holistic experience design.

While I wasn’t at NHS to see direct influence on the website, the prototype helped create the conditions for deeper change. It led to the commissioning of further work — including the initiative covered in my <i>Service Design Adoption</i> case study — where we were embedded into service teams to facilitate mapping, alignment, and a more systemic design approach.

In that sense, the outcome was bigger than the prototype itself: it helped shift the narrative.

---

## Key learnings

This project taught me that not all prototypes are meant to be tested. Some are made to start conversations — and that’s just as valuable. By embracing the idea of prototyping to provoke, we helped senior leaders see beyond the silos and start to imagine what a connected, user-focused NHS.uk might look like.

I also learned how critical it is to make invisible problems visible. By showing what a joined-up experience could look like — grounded in real user context — we helped leadership see the disconnect in their current approach. That shift in perspective laid the groundwork for deeper organisational change.

Finally, I saw the value of working quickly and collectively with clear framing. Having established design principles, co-created journeys, and regular critique gave structure to ambiguity and allowed us to deliver something credible in just six weeks — a pace that wouldn’t have been possible without trust, alignment, and shared intent.
