### We knew the service had a problem, but where?

<Columns>

<Column>

## Overview

As the lead UX Designer, I was responsible for the redesign and delivery of a large, business-critical Government service used by 150,000 internal users, performing over 40 million transactions annually.

### Challenge

<Challenge>

### To reduce password-related service desk calls by improving the clarity and usability of DVSA’s sign-in flows.

</Challenge>

The sign-in process was responsible for a high volume of calls to the service desk, signalling confusion and friction in a critical daily task for 150,000 users. Although these issues were often logged under “password problems,” early investigation revealed that confusion extended across multiple parts of the authentication journey.

Addressing the root cause meant untangling a complex mix of UI patterns, terminology, and user expectations.

**Goal:**25% reduction in password-related service desk calls

## Approach

### Investigating the problem

We began by validating the categorisation of service desk calls, recognising that call handlers' discretion might lead to inconsistencies. To gain firsthand insight, we scheduled research visits to the service desk to listen to calls and understand user frustrations directly.

<Figure src="/assets/projects/improving-dvsas-sign-in-experience/sign-in-artefact-1.png" />

### Root cause analysis

Given the tight timeline, I facilitated a "5 Whys" workshop to delve into potential causes of sign-in issues. This collaborative session expanded our focus beyond passwords, identifying nine user journeys across approximately 60 screens and three communication channels that required investigation.

### Mapping user journeys

I mapped all experience flows related to setting and answering security questions or passwords. This visualisation helped me understand the current state and identify potential areas of ambiguity or inconsistency that could confuse users.

<Figure src="/assets/projects/improving-dvsas-sign-in-experience/sign-in-artefact-2.png" />

### Quantitative and qualitative research

We analysed Google Analytics data to assess user behavior across the identified flows, identifying high-traffic areas with low conversion rates. Concurrently, we synthesised qualitative insights from service desk interactions, validating our quantitative findings and highlighting specific problem areas.

### Heuristic evaluation

Due to time constraints, we couldn't conduct additional usability studies. Instead, another interaction designer and I performed heuristic evaluations using Nielsen's 10 principles. We independently reviewed the flows, compared analyses, and documented 32 opportunities for improvement across six themes:

1. Clarifying security questions
2. Preventing accidental input
3. Facilitating error recovery
4. Eliminating dead ends
5. Reducing cognitive load
6. Providing clear task confirmations

### Prioritising and implementing solutions

I translated the 32 recommendations into user stories, each with associated metrics to test our hypotheses. Collaborating with the development team, we refined and prioritised these stories based on research evidence, development effort, and potential synergies, ensuring a focused and efficient implementation process.

We achieved the business objective of a 25% reduction in password related service desk calls in three development sprints. Rather than abandoning development on reaching its goal, the organisation choose to complete the backlog of work in order to realise additional operational savings. By the end of the development, password-related calls to the service desk were reduced by over 50%.

## Key learnings

This work reinforced how valuable it is to challenge assumptions informed by metrics. What looked like “password issues” on the surface turned out to be a much broader usability problem — one that spanned journeys, channels, and mental models. It reminded me that categorised data only tells part of the story, and listening directly to users (and those supporting them) reveals so much more nuance.

I also saw the power of visualising complexity. Mapping user journeys, even when rough or incomplete, helped others see the problem more clearly — and gave our team confidence to take action.

Finally, it showed me that you don’t always need more research — just smarter analysis. By combining analytics, journey mapping, call listening, and heuristic evaluation, we were able to diagnose and act quickly, without sacrificing insight.
